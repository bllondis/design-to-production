<Section label="Problem to solve" title="From manual recall to review and confirmation">
  Timesheet entry often asks people to reconstruct work after it has already happened. The product hypothesis was
  simple: much of that context already exists across calendars and work tools. Instead of asking users to remember
  everything, Timeflow could assemble available signals into something they review and confirm.
</Section>

<Section label="Core positioning" title="Designing the trust layer for AI-assisted timesheets">
  Timeflow is designed around AI-assisted reconstruction of work activity. The deployed MVP already proves the signal
  and integration layer with real Google Calendar data, OAuth, database infrastructure, and source-attributed entries.
  The next technical layer is LLM-assisted structuring and classification.
</Section>

<Section label="Research & strategy" title="Finding the real signal">
  The exploration converged on three product-shaping hypotheses: reconstructing a day creates unnecessary memory work;
  automation can easily feel like surveillance; and trust requires control, source transparency, and review before
  submission.
</Section>

<Section label="Daily confirmation · The core screen" title="Review your day - don't log it">
  The core interaction reverses the traditional timesheet model. Instead of starting with an empty form, the user starts
  with a reviewable draft assembled from available work signals. Each entry preserves its source and is designed to
  support confidence information, so the user can verify, edit, and submit rather than reconstruct the day from memory.
</Section>

<Section
  label="Data & privacy · Trust is the product"
  title="How do you automate work tracking without making it feel like surveillance?"
>
  The privacy concept makes connected sources and permissions part of the product surface. People should be able to
  understand which signals Timeflow can access, turn integrations on or off, and review every proposed entry before
  submission. The working MVP proves the Google Calendar connection and source visibility. The broader permissions model
  and additional integrations shown here are product direction.
</Section>

<Section label="Where it got complicated" title="Three problems worth keeping">
  Building Timeflow end-to-end as a solo designer-developer surfaced a different kind of problem-solving than working in
  a team. Three moments shaped the product most.
</Section>

<Cards columns={3}>
  <Card title="The OAuth 404 chain">
    After successful Google authorization, the callback kept returning 404. **The redirect URIs were correct.** The real
    cause was a missing UNIQUE constraint on the integrations table, which made the Supabase upsert fail silently.
    Diagnosed by reading raw Auth logs, not guessing.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Color overload discovery">
    A screenshot review of the Manager View revealed **six simultaneous orange elements** competing for attention. A
    systematic audit cut it back to the three sanctioned uses. Everything else moved to neutral so the hierarchy felt
    calm again.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Trust vs. automation">
    The central product tension was how to make future AI suggestions useful without encouraging blind acceptance.
    **Source attribution, confidence, and explicit user confirmation** became core interaction principles.
  </Card>
</Cards>

<Section label="Results" title="Working product. Real data.">
  In four weeks, Timeflow moved from concept to a deployed working MVP. The application includes authentication, database infrastructure, and an end-to-end Google Calendar integration. Real Calendar events can be retrieved and surfaced inside the product with source attribution.

This validates the signal and infrastructure layer needed for the larger product vision. The next milestone is adding AI-assisted structuring so those signals can become reviewable timesheet suggestions.
